## Citation
Oh, Yoo Jung, Junwen M. Hu, Rui Zhu, Jong In Lim, and Xinyu Zhang (2026). Artificial Intelligence Chatbots as Relational Agents: A Systematic Review of Human–AI Chatbot Relationships. International Journal of Human–Computer Interaction. Published online April 16, 2026. https://doi.org/10.1080/10447318.2026.2648799

## Accessible research story
AI chatbots are more than tools for delivering information: people can experience trust, support, and a sense of connection in their conversations with them. This review examines how these relationships develop and how they may help explain chatbots' social influence.

## Central questions (paper section 1.4)
What characteristics of chatbots, users, and messages may influence human–AI chatbot relationships? What are the potential social influence outcomes of those relationships?

## Method and sample (sections 2 and 3.1)
A PRISMA-guided systematic review with qualitative synthesis, not a new participant experiment and not a meta-analysis. Searched six databases: ACM Digital Library, Web of Science, Scopus, IEEE, EBSCO, and PsycINFO. The search identified 16,358 records; 68 papers encompassing 78 studies met inclusion criteria. Eligible peer-reviewed English-language journal articles and conference proceedings were published January 2010 through December 2023. No new human participant data were collected. Do not present 78 as a participant sample or give an invented pooled participant total.

## Main findings (abstract and discussion sections 4.1–4.4)
- Trust and perceived social support frequently functioned as relational mediators linking chatbot, user, and message characteristics to social influence outcomes.
- Chatbot characteristics were examined most frequently; message-level characteristics such as empathy and responsiveness were less studied despite associations with relational outcomes.
- Human-likeness consistently predicted trust in the reviewed literature, but findings were mixed for other relational outcomes. Do not claim human-likeness always creates stronger relationships.
- The literature was dominated by short-term experiments and Western samples, with inconsistent definitions and measurements of key constructs, limiting understanding of long-term and cross-cultural relationship development.

## Why it matters and implications (sections 4.5–5)
The proposed human–AI chatbot Relationship (HAIR) Framework of Social Influence treats relational perceptions and behaviors as pathways between user/chatbot/message characteristics and socially influenced perceptions and behaviors. For researchers, this integrates interpersonal communication with human-computer interaction. For designers, it points beyond technical performance toward trust, rapport, responsiveness, and ethically enacted empathy, disclosure, and personalization. Relational benefits and risks can coexist: trust and perceived support may also increase overreliance. These are implications, not proof that any particular chatbot intervention works.

## Caution
Heterogeneous measures prevented meta-analysis; findings were not weighted or restricted by study quality. Short-term designs, uneven reporting and sampling, inconsistent constructs, and restricted English-language peer-reviewed coverage limit generalizability and causal certainty. More longitudinal, real-world, cross-cultural work is needed.

## General-audience takeaway
Understanding AI's influence means looking not only at what a chatbot says, but also at the relationship people experience while talking with it.
