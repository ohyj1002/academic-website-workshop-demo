1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots are conversational agents that have the ability to continuously and
naturally converse, without a pre-set script (Rapp et al., 2021). A growing body of research highlights
their potential for social influence across diverse domains, including support provision (Beattie & High,
2022); health behavior promotion (Oh et al., 2021), business (Mariani et al., 2023), and education (Guo
& Wang, 2025; Kuhail et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). Despite the increasing use of chatbots across
diverse social influence contexts, chatbots are often framed in primarily instrumental terms, as tools for
delivering information and executing human commands. Communication research, however, empha­
sizes that influence unfolds within the context of personal relationships (Katz & Lazarsfeld, 1955),
pointing to the need to consider chatbots not only as functional tools but also as relational partners.
   Bickmore and Picard (2005) conceptualized computational programs with which people can form
personal relationships as relational agents. They argued that a central step in leveraging these agents for
social influence is supporting users in building such relationships. Advances in AI, machine learning,
and natural language processing (Brandtzaeg & Følstad, 2017) have since made it possible for AI chat­
bots to engage in increasingly natural and humanlike conversations. These technological developments
have expanded chatbots’ roles beyond task completion, enhancing their relational capabilities and pav­
ing the way for more complex human–chatbot interactions. Recent research underscores the potential
of human–AI chatbot relationships, revealing that humans can develop relationships with chatbots in a
similar way to how they establish relationships with other humans, characterized by multiple

nectedness and closeness (Christoforakos et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2020), and companionship (Skjuve

tionship building through both interpersonal concepts, such as trust, and pseudo-personal concepts,
such as anthropomorphism (for reviews, see Ng & Zhang, 2025, Rapp et al., 2021). Prior reviews have
primarily adopted an HCI or user-experience lens, emphasizing themes such as satisfaction, engage­
ment, acceptance, emotional involvement, and perceived humanness (Rapp et al., 2021). Other synthe­
ses have narrowed their focus to a single relational construct, most notably trust, examining how it is
defined, operationalized, and predicted (Ng & Zhang, 2025). While these efforts provide valuable
insights, they do not systematically examine how relational mechanisms function as mediating pathways
linking chatbot characteristics to downstream social influence outcomes. Consequently, a relationship-
centered, theory-driven synthesis remains needed.
   Studies suggest that AI chatbots that are perceived as personal contacts can have unique advantages
in persuasion tasks such as reducing psychological reactance to social influence through relational com­
munication, as compared to other types of AI persuasion technology (e.g., contemplator AI technolo­
gies which help make decisions such as AI doctors). Central to these are similar attributional processes
like in relational interaction between humans, which suggests that relational communication theories
can be applied to understanding how human–AI chatbot relationships are built and the impact on
social influence (Bickmore & Picard, 2005). In this study, we define relationship building outcomes as
perceptual and behavioral outcomes within users indicating how well users and AI chatbots are con­
nected. Bickmore and Picard (2005) idea of relational agents leads us to propose the Human–AI
Chatbot Relationship (HAIR) Framework of Social Influence. Centering the role of relational communi­
cation, the HAIR framework highlights how communication may foster organic, personal relationships
between humans and AI chatbots, thereby shaping user perceptions, emotions, and behaviors. While
the idea of social influence through personal relationships with AI chatbots is promising, existing find­
ings have yet to be systematically synthesized under such a framework. Here, we present a timely sys­
tematic review under the HAIR framework. Specifically, we review contributing factors to human–AI
chatbot relationships (relational outcomes), as well as how human–AI chatbot relationships predict
social influence outcomes. In reviewing the contributors to relational outcomes from a communication
perspective, we review findings relevant to user and chatbot characteristics that may impact communi­
cation processes as well as findings of the features of communication messages exchanged.


1.2. Antecedents of human–AI chatbot relationships: User, chatbot, and message
Existing research on human–AI chatbot relationships has primarily focused on the characteristics of
chatbot users and the chatbots themselves. In terms of user characteristics, prior studies have examined
the impact of demographic traits (e.g., Ki et al., 2020), prior experiences with AI chatbots, attitudes
toward AI (e.g., Gnewuch et al., 2022), personal disposition to trust technology (Prakash et al., 2023),
user personality (e.g., neuroticism: Meng & Dai, 2021), and the motivations and goals behind interac­
tions (e.g., Brandtzaeg & Følstad, 2017) on human–AI chatbot relationships. Regarding chatbot charac­
teristics, research has explored whether factors such as displayed gender (Zogaj et al., 2023), the degree
of humanness or anthropomorphism (Jiang et al., 2023; Li & Sung, 2021; Youn & Jin, 2021), communi­
cation competence (Meng et al., 2023), responsiveness (Jiang et al., 2022), and the type of relationship
formed (Youn & Jin, 2021) affect relational perceptions and behaviors.
   However, beyond user and chatbot characteristics, message characteristics represent a critical yet
often overlooked factor in the development and maintenance of human–AI chatbot relationships.
Communication, at its core, involves the exchange of messages (Lasswell, 1948). The predominant mes­
sage-centered approach by Burleson (2010) contends that interpersonal communication research should
focus on studying messages exchanged in interpersonal interactions to understand how relationships
are built, maintain, and function. The focus on messages highlights the pivotal role of message produc­
tion and interpretation within social interactions, shaping both relational dynamics and broader social
behaviors, thus offering precise insights for the construction of more favorable social outcomes
(Burleson, 2010). The characteristics of messages exchanged between human and AI chatbots can play

a pivotal role in relationship development with AI chatbots (reciprocity: Meng & Dai, 2021; contin­
gency: Meng et al., 2023). However, research that centers on the influence of message characteristics on
building human–AI chatbot relationships remains relatively sparse. Meng and Dai (2021) investigated
how a chatbot’s self-disclosure and provision of emotional support impact individuals’ stress and worry.
Their findings revealed that reciprocal self-disclosure reduced worry, and self-disclosure could alleviate
stress, but only when accompanied by emotional support. Other investigations into message characteris­
tics have delved into the emotionality of the messages (Jiang et al., 2023), support and empathy
expressed in messages (Lee et al., 2022), message contingency (Meng et al., 2023), disfluency (B€   uhrke
et al., 2021), and the level of personalization of the messages (Kim & Hur, 2024). Taken together, in
addition to reviewing findings of user and AI chatbot characteristics that may impact communication
processes and outcomes, we also review findings about communication message characteristics—all
three are contributing factors of human–AI chatbot relationships.


1.3. Human–AI chatbot relationships and social influence outcomes
Recent literature examined the associations between human–AI chatbot communication and subsequent
perceptual and behavioral outcomes (Meng & Dai, 2021; Park et al., 2022). We delineate these out­
comes into two overarching categories: primary (relational) and secondary (social influence) outcomes.
Primary outcomes are those directly tied to the immediate human–AI chatbot relationship. Specific
message exchanges such as self-disclosure or emotional support forms human users’ perceptions of
intimacy (Park, Chueng, & Lee, Lee et al., 2023), trust (Chen et al., 2025), relational satisfaction (Jiang
et al., 2022), and emotional bonding (Lee et al., 2022) with the AI chatbot they interact as well as their
level of engagement. Also, message characteristics have the potential to elicit behavioral reactions,
including subsequent self-disclosure of personal details as well as the expression of a range of emotions.
These perceptual (e.g., rapport) and behavioral (e.g., self-disclosure) outcomes are classified as relational
outcomes. The quality of a human–AI chatbot relationship can vary from nearly non-existent to highly
intense and interdependent.
   Interpersonal scholars contend that relational communication can strongly shape social influence out­
comes (Dillard & Knobloch, 2011). Extending this idea, it is important to consider how human–AI chat­
bot communication may enhance or limit such outcomes. Prior studies have shown that favorable
relational outcomes from human–AI chatbot communication are associated with improved psychological
processes, such as reattributions and reappraisals of stressors (Meng et al., 2023), lower perceived stress
(Meng & Dai, 2021), better emotional experience (Crolic et al., 2022), and higher self-efficacy (Croes &
Antheunis, 2021). Studies have also found that relational outcomes are linked to secondary behavioral
outcomes, such as adherence to health guidelines (Park, Chung, & Lee, 2022), educational achievement
(De Nieva et al., 2020), and purchasing behaviors (Croes & Antheunis, 2021).


1.4. Overview of the current study
Drawing upon the aforementioned literature, we propose the HAIR framework of social influence (see
Figure 1), to guide our systematic review. Briefly summarized, we see human–AI chatbot relationship
building as the bridge between antecedents (i.e., characteristics of users, AI chatbots, and the messages
exchanged) and social influence outcomes. Our review focuses closely on relationship-related variables
as key mechanisms in this process. Based on this framework, we pose the following research questions.

RQ1: What characteristics of (a) chatbot, (b) user, and (c) message may influence human–AI chatbot
 relationships?
RQ2: What are potential social influence outcomes of human–AI chatbot relationships?

2.1. Eligibility criteria
The eligibility of studies was determined by the following criteria: Study aims, outcomes, chatbot sys­
tem feature, modality, study design, source type, publication date, and language (Table 1). For this
review, we included studies on AI chatbots with explicit AI features, focusing on those that primarily
deliver messages via text or speech, with emphasis on the human-AI interaction and its impacts.
Eligible studies include cross-sectional, quasi-experimental, and randomized controlled trials, published
in English in peer-reviewed journals or conference proceedings from January 1st, 2010, to December
31, 2023. We limited inclusion to 2010 onward to focus on modern AI-driven conversational agents
capable of sustained and socially interactive communication, as earlier systems were primarily rule-
based and less suitable for examining relational processes. This criterion aligned with prior reviews
(e.g., Rapp et al., 2021).

2.2. Information sources
A systematic search was conducted on six electronic interfaces, including ACM Digital Library, Web of
Science Core Collection (WOS), SCOPUS, IEEE, EBSCO, and PsycINFO. References of the selected
studies were checked to identify other eligible studies. Any additional potentially eligible studies were
identified and screened. References were exported into the online software Covidence (www.covidence.
org), and duplicates were removed using this tool.


2.3. Search strategy
The search query was a combination of chatbot-related keywords and relationship-related keywords.
After reviewing past literature, the search terms were thoroughly examined and selected by the team. In
each database, this search query was used to identify literature containing the search terms in the title
or abstract. Detailed search strategies can be found in Appendix B.


2.4. Study selection
All retrieved references were reported into the Endnote reference management software (https://end­
note.com), and duplicates were removed. The remaining references were imported into the Covidence
systematic review software (http://www.covidence.org), and additional duplicates were removed. Before
screening the articles, four researchers met to discuss the procedure for title and abstract screening
using 20 randomly selected papers. Then, two researchers imported approximately 20% of total referen­
ces for dual screening of titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. The agreement in the abstract
and title screening between two reviewers was 96.7% (Krippendorff’s alpha ¼ 0.72). Then, the remain­
ing abstract and title screening were done independently. Then, they both reviewed 25% of the full
text. The agreement was 94.6% (Krippendorff’s alpha ¼ 0.86). After achieving a good level of inter-rater
reliability, the remaining full texts were split into two and independently assessed. Discrepancies at
each stage were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

2.5. Data collection process
Data extraction was conducted by two independent reviewers. Data collection forms were developed
based on the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and the PRISMA strategy
(Higgins et al., 2019). The data items to be extracted were pilot-tested using a Microsoft Excel spread­
sheet and a sample of potential studies obtained through the initial iterative search process. A third
reviewer was involved in resolving any disagreements and cross-checked the accuracy of the extracted
data.


2.6. Data items
The data extraction form includes study characteristics (e.g., author, published year, study design, set­
ting, and duration), participant characteristics (e.g., age, gender, education, sample size, race, and socio-
economic status), study design (e.g., survey or experiment), artificial intelligence under investigation
(e.g., virtual companion, and chatbots), antecedents (e.g., characteristics of messages, AI chatbots, and
users), secondary outcomes (e.g., health behavioral change), and safety and ethical assessment.


2.7. Data synthesis
We conducted a qualitative synthesis of findings from eligible studies, covering characteristics of AI
chatbots, study designs, target population, and primary and secondary outcomes. Regarding risk of bias
assessment, the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT; Hong et al., 2018) was used to evaluate the
quality of the selected studies. Consistent with prior MMAT-based systematic reviews that summarize
study rigor using percentage thresholds (e.g., Chu et al., 2020; El-Awaisi et al., 2018), studies meeting at
least four or five criteria (� 80%) were considered to be ‘high quality’. We first selected 10 studies and
all authors independently coded and cross-validated the coding through multiple rounds of discussion
until we reached consensus. Then, we divided the rest of the studies to be assessed independently.


3. Results
3.1. Study selection
Figure 2 shows the study selection process. The search yielded 16,358 references in total, from which
8,631 duplicates were removed. A total of 7,727 abstracts were initially screened, among which 7,357
were deemed to be ineligible or manually identified as duplicates, leaving 370 to be read in full text. In
total 68 papers (and 78 corresponding studies) have been included for review.


3.2. Risk of bias assessment
Seventy-eight studies were assessed using the MMAT (see Appendix C). We identified 48 quantitative
randomized controlled trials, 2 quantitative non-randomized studies, and 27 quantitative descriptive
studies. Among the studies, 8 randomized controlled trials, 1 non-randomized study, and 20 quantita­
tive descriptive studies were regarded as high quality. Studies satisfying at least 80% (four or five)
assessment criteria were considered as high quality. For quantitative randomized controlled trials, the
criterion “Are the groups comparable at baseline?” was most frequently rated as unclear, with 83.3% of
studies lacking sufficient reporting of baseline characteristics across groups. For quantitative descriptive
studies, the most common limitation concerned sample representativeness, with 82.1% of studies rated
as “No” or “Cannot tell” for whether the sample reflected the target population. These findings indicate
that many studies did not adequately report baseline comparability or justify how samples represented
their intended populations.

3.3. Descriptives of studies
Table 2 summarizes descriptives of studies. Among the 78 included studies, about two-thirds used
experimental designs (primarily online), roughly one-third employed surveys, and only a handful were
content analyses. They were predominantly published between 2021 and 2023, with sample sizes rang­
ing from under 100 to over 500 participants. Studies commonly focused on specific groups (e.g., stu­
dents or particular language/experience). Geographically, most studies were conducted in Europe or the
United States, although a substantial share also involved Asian countries. The studies investigated a
range of chatbot functions, most commonly generalist assistants (n ¼ 27), social (n ¼ 19), and transac­
tional (n ¼ 17) chatbots, with fewer focusing on health (n ¼ 10) or educational (n ¼ 4) purposes. In
terms of investigated antecedents, chatbot-related factors (n ¼ 46) were examined most frequently, fol­

3.5. Trust
We first review outcomes that are categorized as trust. Trust has heterogeneous definitions and opera­
tionalizations, addressing different domains (e.g., general, ability, benevolence; Peng & Wan, 2024) and
10     Y. J. OH ET AL.



distinct evaluative aspects (e.g., affective vs. cognitive; Chen et al., 2025). That said, trust is often
defined as a tendency to believe in AI chatbots in fulfilling their roles and meeting user expectations in
the studies reviewed.
   For chatbot-related predictors, various indicators of human-likeliness (e.g., anthropomorphism; Chen
et al., 2025; Li et al., 2023) and social presence in general (e.g., Prakash et al., 2023) were consistently
associated with higher trust. Some but not all indicators of competencies (e.g., cognitive, and emotional,
Chandra et al., 2022) or general technology experiences (e.g., Prakash et al., 2023) were positively linked
to trust outcomes; some were negatively related to trust (e.g., errors; Kim et al., 2023).
   For communication-related predictors, quality and supportiveness (empathy: Liao & Yan, 2023;
responsiveness; Li et al., 2023) were positively related to higher trust whereas speed of delivering
inaccurate information (Amaro et al., 2024) was negatively related to trust. As for user-related predic­
tors, propensity to trust was positively associated with trust (Prakash et al., 2023), but privacy concern
was not consistently significantly related to trust (Li et al., 2023). Relational and context factors were
also related to trust: higher trusting beliefs (Prakash et al., 2023) and higher intimacy (Qiao et al.,
2022) were associated with more trust, and higher negative impression (Kim et al., 2023) and task com­
plexity (Casadei et al., 2022) were associated with lower trust.
   Across studies, relational trust is primarily driven by socio-emotional and social connection factors.
For example, relational trust tends to increase when chatbots convey empathy, foster psychological
safety, feel authentic, and promote relational perceptions such as intimacy. Instrumental trust, on the
other hand, depends more on chatbots’ task performance and reliability. It increases with usefulness,
ease of use, and response and communication quality, and it decreases in the presence of errors and
misinformation. Several predictors are associated with both trust types (e.g., anthropomorphism and
social cues), suggesting that design features highlighting the chatbot as a social agent can strengthen
trust both relationally and instrumentally.


3.6. Relationship strength
Compared with trust, fewer studies examined relationship strength. In this review, relationship strength
refers to the emotional closeness of human–AI chatbot relationships, encompassing outcomes such as
communal bonding perception (Tschopp et al., 2023) and parasocial relationship perception (Hoffman
et al., 2021). Chatbot features related to human-likeliness (e.g., AI sociability; Pentina et al., 2023; Xie
et al., 2023), social presence (e.g., voice; Tschopp et al., 2023), competence (AI supportiveness; Leite
et al., 2013), were associated with stronger relationships between users and AI chatbots.
Communication-wise, longer and more intense interactions (Christoforakos et al., 2021), as well as
more display of attachment behaviors (Xie et al., 2023), were associated with stronger relationships. In
contrast, having more interactions where chatbots asked extensive questions (Hoffman et al., 2021) was
associated with weaker relationship strength. At the user level, older individuals (Tschopp et al., 2023)
and those motivated to interact with chatbots socially (Pentina et al., 2023) were more likely to develop
stronger relationships with chatbots.


3.7. Self-disclosure
Self-disclosure, as a behavioral relational outcome, refers to the act of revealing personal or private
information to AI chatbot (e.g., Croes et al., 2023). Compared with trust, studies on self-disclosure
revealed a different pattern of predictors. Chatbot-level predictors, particularly competence-related cues
produced mixed findings (e.g., Gieselmann & Sassenberg, 2023), whereas communication-level predic­
tors (e.g., frequency of questions asked by chatbots, first-person pronouns; Croes et al., 2023) were
positively related to self-disclosure. Chatbot’s self-disclosure, however, was not significantly related to
user self-disclosure (Croes et al., 2023). Rapport (Baek & Kim, 2023) and relational use (Xu & Li, 2024)
of chatbots were positively related to self-disclosure.

3.8. Aggression
Only a small number of studies examined aggression as a relational outcome. Studies revealed that
lower levels of human-likeliness in chatbots were associated with higher aggression (Keijsers et al.,
2021). Message features that had higher meaning (Keijsers et al., 2021) and morality (Brendel et al.,
2020) were linked to lower aggression. Lastly, more user frustration (Brendel et al., 2023) was associated
with higher aggression. For detailed descriptions for each study, refer to Appendix D2a and D2b.


3.9. Human–AI chatbot relationship and social influence outcomes
In the HAIR framework (Figure 1), secondary outcomes refer to socially influenced perceptions and
socially influenced behaviors that occur downstream of relational mediators. In Tables 5–7, antecedents
(X) map onto user, chatbot, or message characteristics, mediators (M) map onto relational perceptions
or behaviors, and secondary outcomes (Y) map onto socially influenced perceptions or behaviors. We
summarize each pathway using this structure to make the link to the framework explicit.

3.9.1. Perceptual secondary outcomes
Table 5 summarizes mediation pathways in which relational perceptions link antecedents to socially
influenced perceptions. Specifically, Meng and Dai (2021) found that perceived supportiveness of chat­
bots mediated the effect of emotional support on worry reduction and stress reduction. In a study by
Cho et al. (2022), perceptions of active listening by a chatbot mediated the effect of backchanneling on
users’ enthusiastic emotional states. Furthermore, this study explored serial mediation effects and found
that active listening backchanneling influenced perceived usability through perceived active listening
and perceived emotional support but not through the serial mediation of perceived active listening and
positive emotional words. Similarly, Park, Lee, and Chung (2023) found that social rapport perceptions
mediated the effect of chatbot anthropomorphism on both counseling satisfaction and reuse intention.

3.9.2. Behavioral secondary outcomes
Tables 6 and 7 summarize mediation pathways in which relational perceptions link antecedents to
socially influenced behaviors. Trust was the most frequently examined mediator. Trust was most fre­
quently studied relational mediator. Prakash et al. (2023) reported that trusting beliefs mediated the
effects of perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, social presence, and propensity to trust technology
on users’ intention to continue interacting with chatbots. However, trust did not mediate the effect of
privacy risk on continuance intention. Similarly, Qiao et al. (2022) demonstrated that trust mediated
the effects of perceived intimacy on both the intention to utilize the chatbot and the intention to
explore. Baek and Kim (2023) found that trust mediated the effects of information-seeking and task
efficacy on continuance intention, but it did not mediate the effects of personalization, social inter­
action, or playfulness.

   Other studies reported indirect effects via trust or related relational mediators for outcomes. Kim
et al. (2023) examined the serial mediation effects of perceived trustworthiness and satisfaction with
recommendations in relation to salience of ChatGPT issues. They found statistically significant serial
mediation effects for the relationship between positive vs. negative ethical issues and users’ intention to
visit recommended destinations, as well as between positive vs. negative quality issues and visit inten­
tion. Similarly, Park et al. (2022) found that trust mediated the relationship between human-like repre­
sentation (vs. machine-like) and intention to comply with chatbot recommendations. Additionally, a
significant serial mediation effect was observed, where trust and psychological distance mediated the
relationship between human-like representation and intention to comply. However, Liew et al. (2023)
found that trust did not mediate the effects of perceived intelligence of AI or anthropomorphism on
users’ intention to adopt sexual and reproductive health information chatbots.
   Beyond trust, additional relational mediators were identified, including psychological distance, social
attraction, emotional credibility, perceived knowledge, and perceived empathy, linking antecedents to
behavioral outcomes such as compliance intention, purchase intention, and call length (Table 7). Park
and colleagues (2022) found that psychological distance mediated the relationship between human-like
representation and users’ intention to comply with health recommendations. In a study by Lee et al.
(2022), social attraction mediated the associations between emotional support, informational support,
and esteem support with purchase intention. Additionally, emotional credibility was identified as a
mediator for these associations. Finally, Luo et al. (2019) demonstrated that perceived knowledge and
perceived empathy mediated the relationship between the timing of disclosing the chatbot’s identity as
a conversational partner and the length of the call.


4. Discussion
As AI chatbots are evolving rapidly and research on their potential for social influence expands, having
higher-level theoretical frameworks becomes increasingly salient. Despite the surge in studies, many
focus on isolated aspects of chatbot interaction. To address this, we draw upon Bickmore and Picard’s
notion of relational agents (2005) and forward a relationship-centered framework, the HAIR framework
of social influence. This relationship-centered framework guided our systematic review of studies exam­
ining the development of human–AI chatbot relationships. Our findings underscore the multifaceted
nature of human–AI chatbot relationships, with studies spanning diverse contexts and approaches.
Notably, message-level predictors remain underexplored, despite their potential to yield practical
insights for chatbot designs. This synthesis revealed that trust and perceived social support frequently
function as relational mediators through which chatbots exert social influence. Among these, trust
stood out as the most prominent mediator, consistently linked to persuasion outcomes and intention of
continue using the chatbots.


4.1. A human–AI chatbot relationships perspective to social influence
Our review distinguishes itself from prior systematic reviews that examined human–AI chatbot relation­
ships by treating relational perceptions and behaviors not merely as outcomes (e.g., Chaturvedi et al.,
2023; Ng & Zhang, 2025) but as pathways through which chatbots exert social influence. We identified
three primary purposes of chatbot deployment: to gain compliance, provide social support and promote
mental health, and encourage continued chatbot use.
   Across these outcomes, relationship factors such as trust and perceived social support were generally
found to mediate the effects of antecedents, including user, chatbot, and message characteristics. While
scholars often compare human–AI chatbot interactions to interpersonal communication, our synthesis
suggests a more nuanced pattern. Human-likeness or anthropomorphism consistently predicted trust,
yet findings were more mixed for other relational outcomes such as relationship strength, self-disclos­
ure, and aggression. This pattern suggests that human-likeness may be a necessary but not sufficient
condition for developing strong human–AI relationships. Therefore, developing strong relationships
with chatbots should be a central consideration when researchers or designers seek to implement chat­
bots for specific purposes. Indeed, the influence of relational factors is not unique to human–AI
                                                  INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF HUMAN–COMPUTER INTERACTION     15



interactions. Relationship dynamics have long been recognized in social influence research as critical
drivers of persuasion and behavior change (e.g., Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004; Latan�e, 1981).


4.2. Influence of user characteristics
Our results showed that user characteristics can play a meaningful role in shaping human–AI chatbot
relationships. Consistent with broader human-computer interaction research, individual differences
such as propensity to trust emerged as important facilitators of relational outcomes (Prakash et al.,
2023). For instance, users who generally report higher tendencies to trust technology were more likely
to extend trust toward AI chatbots, suggesting that these baseline user inclinations toward technologies
may transfer into human–AI chatbot contexts. User demographics and motivations also shaped rela­
tional outcomes. Age was positively related to relationship strength, with older users more likely to
form close, enduring bonds with chatbots. This aligns with prior findings that older adults may value
companionship and supportive communication in digital interactions, thereby enhancing relational
depth (Tschopp et al., 2023). Similarly, individuals who reported higher social motivations for interact­
ing with chatbots developed stronger relationships, underscoring the importance of understanding user
goals when assessing relational dynamics.
   These findings collectively suggest that human–AI relationships cannot be fully explained by chatbot
design alone. Instead, users’ dispositional traits, demographic factors, and motivational orientations
moderate whether relational communication translates into trust, self-disclosure, or deeper relationship
strength. Future research should further investigate the intersection of these characteristics with chatbot
and communication features, particularly through longitudinal and cross-cultural designs that can cap­
ture the variability and evolution of these relationships across diverse user populations.


4.3. Influence of chatbot’s characteristics
Across all functional categories of chatbots, including educational, social, transactional, health, and gen­
eralist, research consistently prioritizes chatbot-related antecedents. This reflects a prevailing assump­
tion in the field: that a chatbot’s design and core features are the primary determinants of successful
interaction. For educational chatbots, this often translates into an emphasis on pedagogical effective­
ness; for transactional chatbots, efficiency and accuracy are central; for health chatbots, trustworthiness
and personalized support are critical; and for social chatbots, a believable personality and simulated
empathy are important. Even for generalist chatbots, adaptability is itself a design choice.
    This strong focus on chatbot design suggests several implications. First, it highlights the dominance
of a technology-centered perspective, where improvements to functionality are expected to drive rela­
tional and behavioral outcomes. Second, it raises the possibility that other antecedents, such as user dis­
positions or communication dynamics, may be undervalued in current research, potentially limiting the
field’s understanding of human–AI relationship building. Third, the emphasis on design reflects a ten­
dency toward short-term, experimental evaluations of isolated features rather than investigations of
how design interacts with user characteristics and relational processes over time. Finally, this pattern
underscores the need for integrative approaches, where design is studied not in isolation but as part of
a broader system in which user characteristics and relational communication are equally central.


4.4. A relational communication perspective to human–AI chatbot relationships
As a central aim of this review, we find that relational mediators, especially trust, generally translated
the effects of chatbot characteristics (e.g., anthropomorphism: Park et al., 2022), user characteristics
(e.g., propensity to trust technology: Prakash et al., 2023), and message characteristics (e.g., emotional
support: Meng & Dai, 2021) to secondary perceptual and behavioral outcomes. This pattern highlights
the value of incorporating relational processes when designing and evaluating chatbots for social influ­
ence. These findings also echo prior work on relationship dynamics in social influence, which positions
the relationship as a critical driver of persuasion and behavior change (e.g., Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004;
Latan�e, 1981).
16     Y. J. OH ET AL.



   This review further expands upon previous systematic reviews by reviewing message-based determi­
nants of human–AI chatbot relationship development. Although relatively few studies have examined
the effects of communication content, those that did consistently found that empathic and responsive
chatbot messages foster stronger bonds between humans and AI (e.g., Cho et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023;
Liao & Yan, 2023), which in turn are linked to various influence outcomes. These findings underscore
that, beyond chatbot characteristics and users’ dispositions, it is important to pay more attention to
communicative exchanges between humans and chatbot. Many constructs that appear to be chatbot-
level attributes are operationalized in prior work as features of conversational turns or interaction
sequences, such as responsiveness, empathic behaviors, and personalization enacted in replies. We
therefore treat these as message characteristics when they are measured or manipulated at the level of
interaction, and reserve chatbot characteristics for stable system or interface properties.
   At the same time, because relationships play central roles in AI chatbot social influence, it may be
promising to apply interpersonal communication theories to human–AI chatbot relationships, while
carefully testing where these theories translate and where they require revision. For example, whether
those of supportive communication that underline importance of responsiveness (Holmstrom & Lim,
2023), can be readily applied to human–AI chatbot communication contexts or not. While early
research on human-machine communication held that humans tend to automatically apply human-
human social scripts to interactions with machines (Reeves & Nass, 1996), more recent work argues
that as people accumulate experience with machines, the scripts guiding human-AI interaction may
have evolved to be different from those guiding human–AI chatbot interaction (Gambino et al., 2020).
If so, users may hold distinct expectations for empathic and responsive chatbot communication com­
pared to interactions with fellow humans. Exploring these social interaction scripts’ differences repre­
sents a promising direction for future research.


4.5. Implications
This review contributes to future research in several ways. First, it offers scholars a systematic guide to
studying how relational processes can be harnessed for social influence. Rather than treating relational
constructs as incidental outcomes, our framework, the HAIR Framework of Social Influence, positions
them as central pathways through which chatbots build personal relationships with users and exert
influence. Future research can build on this framework to design studies that examine how different
antecedents including user, chatbot, and message characteristics shape relational dynamics and, in turn,
behavioral outcomes.
   For chatbot developers, the proposed framework offers design principles for creating chatbots that
serve relational functions across contexts. Beyond technical performance, developers should embed fea­
tures that foster trust, rapport, and responsiveness, with future work exploring how message strategies
such as self-disclosure, personalization, and empathy cues can be operationalized ethically. This distinc­
tion implies two complementary design levels. System- level choices should target stable relational affor­
dances, such as role framing, identity disclosure, modality, and reliability and error handling features,
whereas message-level design should specify how empathy, active listening, contingency, calibrated self-
disclosure, and personalization are enacted in the content of responses. For service providers in sectors
like health and education, our findings highlight that effective outcomes, whether compliance, support,
or sustained engagement, depend on cultivating strong human–chatbot relationships. This relational
leverage also carries risk. Trust and perceived social support may heighten overreliance when chatbot
interaction becomes a primary source of emotional reassurance or guidance. Work on social compan­
ion chatbots documents emotional dependence and related harms (Hu et al., 2023), indicating that rela­
tional benefits and relational risks can coexist (Laestadius et al., 2024). Because most existing studies
are short term, future research should test relationship centered interventions in real world settings
using longitudinal designs and outcome measures that track reliance trajectories, potential displacement
of offline support, and the boundary between supportive engagement and problematic dependence.

4.6. Limitations and future directions
The current review has several limitations that should be noted. First, we restricted our search to peer-
reviewed, full-text studies published in English and did not include gray literature, which may limit the
comprehensiveness of the evidence base. A key assumption of many studies is that AI chatbots are
used to optimize individuals’ hedonic experiences. With this being natural in individualistic cultures,
we wonder whether AI chatbots may also serve collective purposes (e.g., enhancing community solidar­
ity) in collectivist cultures; however, the second aspect is less studied and may be enhanced in future
research. Second, due to the heterogeneity of outcome measures and the limited number of randomized
controlled trials (RCTs), we were unable to conduct a meta-analysis, which constrains our ability to
draw firm conclusions about the effects of AI chatbots on human–AI chatbot relationships. We encour­
age future research to focus on specific relational outcomes, such as trust and self-disclosure, and to
conduct meta-analyses to quantify the magnitude and boundary conditions of these effects as the evi­
dence base continues to grow. Third, the risk of bias assessment identified several recurring methodo­
logical concerns. Many randomized trials did not clearly report baseline comparability across groups,
and a large proportion of descriptive studies relied on samples with limited representativeness. Because
our findings were not weighted or restricted by study quality, conclusions reflect the full range of meth­
odological rigor and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Future work should prioritize stronger
sampling procedures and clearer reporting practices. In addition, many of the included studies exam­
ined interactions of very short duration, making it difficult to draw conclusions about the development
of human–AI chatbot relationships. Because relationships are inherently dynamic and tend to evolve
over time, short-term designs limit our ability to assess whether initial relational perceptions translate
into sustained relationships. Future work should employ longer-term study designs to better capture
the trajectories of relationship building with chatbots.
   This review also highlights key limitations in the current state of research. A primary concern is the
inconsistency, and sometimes absence, of clear conceptual and operational definitions for variables,
including antecedents, mediators, and outcomes. These inconsistencies pose challenges to synthesizing
prior research and accumulating coherent findings. For example, trust can be defined and operational­
ized both generally and multi-dimensionally (Rapp et al., 2021), with different implications depending
on the specific definitions and operationalizations used. As Rapp and his colleagues pointed out,
research has often defined and measured trust in a specific way without sufficient justifications of the
choice, thus making undue generalizations of their findings. In addition, chatbots have been seen both
as service providers or tools (e.g., Adam et al., 2021) and relational agents (Brandtzaeg et al., 2022).
Trust in service providers can be more instrumental, whereas trust in social agents can be more inter­
personal and relational. Failure to distinguish between instrumental and relational trust can lead to
inaccurate generalizations in research syntheses (Rapp et al., 2021). Future research should prioritize
theoretical clarity by explicitly distinguishing between different types of trust (e.g., instrumental vs. rela­
tional) and aligning measurement with these definitions. Doing so will allow findings to be meaning­
fully compared across studies and accumulated into broader theoretical insights.


5. Conclusion
This systematic review synthesizes research on human–AI chatbot relationships, identifying key antece­
dents, relational mediators, and social influence outcomes. It highlights how relationships with chatbots
serve as a central process through which social influence unfolds, with message characteristics playing
an especially understudied role. In doing so, the review informs both theoretical advancement and
practical design of AI chatbots across diverse contexts.
   Future research on human–AI chatbot relationships should explicitly integrate interpersonal commu­
nication theory with human-computer interaction research. Interpersonal communication perspectives
identify message mechanisms that produce relational states, whereas HCI specifies the design and sys­
tem constraints that determine whether these cues support sustained, appropriate use. We outline three
integration pathways. First, construct alignment is needed to enable cumulative evidence, including
clear distinctions between relational trust and instrumental trust and between perceived support and
18     Y. J. OH ET AL.



usability or satisfaction. Second, boundary conditions should be theorized and tested, including how
errors, transparency cues, privacy costs, and prior AI experience shape the transfer of human–human
interaction scripts. Third, methodological integration should link message-level analysis with longitu­
dinal, ecologically valid evaluations that capture relationship trajectories and downstream influence.
This agenda positions the HAIR framework as a cumulative scaffold for explaining when relational
communication promotes beneficial influence versus when it produces miscalibration and overreliance.

About the authors
Yoo Jung (Erika) Oh is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Communication at Michigan State
University. Her research investigates the impact of persuasive AI on human perceptions and behaviors. Dr. Oh’s
research focuses on human perceptions, communication patterns, and the resulting persuasion and relational out­
comes of human-AI interaction.
Junwen M. Hu is an incoming Assistant Professor in the Department of Communication at the University of
California, Davis. He studies how people select and mobilize personal relationships for symbolic and tangible
resources, focusing on online mate selection and social support.
Rui Zhu is a PhD candidate at the Department of Communication, Michigan State University. Her research
focuses on communication technologies and how they shape human relationships.
Jong In Lim is a PhD candidate at the Department of Communication, Michigan State University. His research
interest includes supportive communication and its influence on relational and psychological health.
Xinyu Zhang is a PhD student at the Department of Communication, Michigan State University. Her research
focuses on the impact of communication technologies on interpersonal communication and self-identity.
